Where GenAI can help industries next: three low-hanging fruit

Question set by the course: "What other directions could GenAI help industries? Suggest recommendations and identify three low-hanging-fruit opportunities (few people doing it, highest impact, lowest implementation effort)." Written from market research dated 2025–2026; every figure is referenced to a numbered source in section 6. Where we draw on our own project, we say so.

1. What building ThreadPilot taught us about where value is

ThreadPilot is a general manager's co-pilot for a small knitwear factory: it answers questions over the order book and the production log, writes a morning briefing, raises an alert only when a decision is needed, checks whether a new order can be accepted, and drafts chase-up emails, for stalled orders before anyone asks, that a human confirms. Three design lessons from it carry over to any industry:

  • Grounding beats cleverness. The failures that cost us marks were never wrong arithmetic; they were a model reading a question wrongly or answering an adjacent question. The fix was to let the model only route and word, while code computes from records with a source for every number. That pattern needs no new data collection, which is exactly why it suits firms that have records but no data team.
  • Proactive and rationed. Managers do not open dashboards; they read what arrives. An alert policy with tiers, caps and cooldowns (at most three urgent emails a day) made the system something a person could live with.
  • Actions behind a confirmation. Drafting is cheap; sending is not. A preview and an exact confirmation turned a chat toy into something a manager would let near a customer.

The recommendations below are chosen where these three patterns fit, in industries where the adoption data say few are doing it yet.

2. The landscape in 2026: adoption is broad, value is narrow, and small firms are behind

  • Use is near-universal in large firms, value is not. Nearly nine in ten respondents to McKinsey's 2026 global survey use AI regularly in at least one function and 44% say they are scaling it enterprise-wide, yet only 37% attribute any EBIT impact to it and about 6% qualify as high performers[1]. Stanford's AI Index 2026 puts organisational AI use at 88% and regular generative-AI use at 79% in 2025[2].
  • Operations is where the measurable savings are. McKinsey respondents most often report cost reductions in supply-chain management, service operations and manufacturing; revenue gains cluster in marketing, sales and product development[1]. Deloitte's 2026 survey of 3,235 leaders finds 66% report productivity gains but only 20% are growing revenue from AI[13].
  • The pilot-to-production funnel leaks. MIT's NANDA group estimates that about 95% of organisations see no profit-and-loss return on US$30–40 billion of enterprise generative-AI spend and that only around 5% of custom tools reach production[9]. Gartner forecast that at least 30% of generative-AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, and that more than 40% of agentic-AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027, citing cost, unclear value and weak risk controls[10][11]. Spend rises regardless: Gartner forecasts US$2.59 trillion of AI spending in 2026, up 47%[12].
  • Small and mid-sized firms are far behind, everywhere. United States: 19.8% of firms used AI in May 2026, but 37% of firms with 250+ staff against under 20% of the smallest[3]. European Union: 55.0% of large enterprises use AI versus 30.4% of medium and 17.0% of small ones[5][6]. Singapore: SME adoption tripled from 4.2% (2023) to 14.5% (2024), still far below 62.5% for non-SMEs[7]. The OECD finds the same pattern across the G7[8].
  • Mid-market manufacturing is adopting fast but shallowly. 70.9% of Manufacturing Leadership Council respondents now use generative-AI tools (46% a year earlier); the top use is knowledge management (56.6%) and the top barrier is data issues (62.3%), then an unclear business case (41.5%) and skills (40.2%)[14]. RSM's 2026 middle-market survey: 79% of manufacturers use generative AI, and where pilots fell short the causes were data quality (51%) and integration (43%)[15]. The Federal Reserve notes manufacturing had the fastest growth in worker generative-AI use of any sector in 2025[4].
  • Agents scale with company size. 40% of firms with revenue above US$1 billion are scaling AI agents, against a flat 22% of smaller firms[1]. In logistics only about 10% of service providers have AI embedded at scale and 13% report measurable value[17].
  • The barriers are data readiness, trust and skills, not model capability. Deloitte names worker skills the biggest integration barrier and legacy data the limit on autonomous AI[13]; among 800+ freight forwarders only 23% say most of their data is clean and reliable[18]; the UK civil service's 20,000-licence Copilot trial found the tool weak on nuanced, data-heavy work and dependent on human oversight, while still saving about 26 minutes a day[20].
What this implies. The open ground is not another model or another large-enterprise platform. It is narrow, records-grounded assistants for the functions where savings are already measurable (operations, supply chain, back office), sold to the firms that have the records but not the data team: small and mid-sized manufacturers, forwarders, suppliers and contractors.

4. The three low-hanging fruit

Pick 1 · Manufacturing · Highest on all three criteria

A shop-floor knowledge and shift-handover copilot for mid-market manufacturers

Why few are doing it. Mid-market plants use generative AI mostly through general chat tools; knowledge management is their top use at 56.6%, but packaged industrial copilots are sold to large OEMs, and agent scaling is stuck at 22% in smaller firms against 40% in large ones[14][1]. The segment is adopting (70.9%, up from 46%) without a product built for it.

Why the impact is high. Up to 1.9 million US manufacturing roles may go unfilled by 2033 as experienced workers retire[16]; what leaves with them is the tacit knowledge of why a line stops and what fixed it last time. Manufacturing is one of the functions where survey respondents most often report AI cost savings[1]. Capturing "this fault recurred on Line 3 twice this week, here is what was done last time" lifts output and quality directly.

Why the effort is low. It needs only records the plant already keeps: maintenance logs, defect sheets, SOP PDFs, shift notes. Retrieval with citations over those records, plus a recurrence alert, is a first version; no sensors, no MES rewrite. The dominant barrier in the segment, data issues at 62.3%[14], is met by grounding answers in cited records rather than by training anything.

Relation to ThreadPilot: the same records-grounded question answering and the same proactive-alert policy, pointed at maintenance and quality records instead of orders. Risk: a wrong instruction on a safety-critical task; mitigate with citations on every answer and a human confirmation before any work order is raised.

Pick 2 · Logistics and factory sales · Strongest causal evidence

An order-to-quote inbox agent for small forwarders, 3PLs and factory sales desks

Why few are doing it. Only about 10% of logistics service providers have AI embedded at scale and 1% of shippers, while nearly half intend to deploy it in quoting and customer service: a gap between intent and execution[17].

Why the impact is high. 37% of apparel and fashion customers already expect AI-enabled forwarders[17]. The strongest causal evidence for generative-AI productivity anywhere is in exactly this task type: a study of 5,179 support agents found +14% productivity overall and +34% for less experienced staff[19]; the UK government trial's largest saving was drafting, about 24 minutes a day[20].

Why the effort is low. A shared mailbox, a rate sheet or TMS export, and a draft-then-confirm loop. No ERP replacement. The agent reads the enquiry, checks capacity and rates against records, drafts the quote or status reply, and a person confirms before it leaves.

Relation to ThreadPilot: an extension of the confirmed-action pattern already demonstrated with chase-up emails. Risk: mis-quotes; only 23% of forwarders trust most of their data[18], which argues for confirm-before-send rather than autonomy.

Pick 3 · Apparel and textile suppliers · Nobody is serving the supplier side

Compliance and traceability pack assembly for apparel and textile SME suppliers

Why few are doing it. Digital Product Passport and traceability tooling is aimed at brands and importers; the tier-2 knitwear and garment suppliers who must supply the data are largely unserved, and retailers themselves warn the requirements are particularly burdensome for SMEs[28][29].

Why the impact is high. This is market access, not efficiency. The textile delegated act under the EU Ecodesign regulation is indicatively due in Q4 2027[28]; buyers are already shifting sourcing under tariff pressure, which fashion executives rank as their top hurdle[30]. A supplier that cannot produce certificates and fibre-composition data on request loses the order.

Why the effort is moderate. Bills of materials, supplier certificates and test reports already exist as files; the work is extraction, template filling and gap alerts ("this yarn lot has no OEKO-TEX certificate on file"). It scores lower than the first two on effort because the regulatory detail is still moving, but highest on "nobody is doing it".

Relation to ThreadPilot: it reuses the feasibility check ("can we accept this order?" now also asks "do we hold the compliance evidence?") and the alert policy for expiring certificates, with a confirmed action to request documents from yarn suppliers. Risk: errors in a passport carry liability; keep a human sign-off and a source for every field.

Runner-up. Construction RFI and change-order drafting (D) is as low in effort as the first two picks, but the field is filling faster (one in three professionals already using AI)[24], so it scores lower on "few doing it".

5. Recommendations for anyone building these

  1. Start from records the firm already keeps, and cite them. Data readiness is the first barrier in every survey[13][14][15][18]. A system that answers from existing files with a source for every number needs no data programme to start, and earns trust faster than one that asks for clean data first.
  2. Keep the model to reading and wording; let code compute. Our own evaluation found zero untraced numbers in 169 stated and all failures in routing, not arithmetic; on 70 fresh held-out questions the misses clustered in two missing tools, not in reasoning. That division is also what makes the system auditable, which the 40% agentic-project cancellation forecast[11] suggests buyers will demand.
  3. Ration the interruptions. Alerts with tiers, daily caps and cooldowns are the difference between a tool that is read and one that is muted. Measure usefulness ratings, not alert counts.
  4. Put every outward action behind a preview and a confirmation. It costs one tap and removes the failure that ends adoption. Autonomy can be earned later, per action type, from the confirmation log.
  5. Measure first-pass accuracy on questions written by someone else, and publish the failures. The MIT and Gartner figures[9][10] describe pilots that could not show value; a blind question sheet in week one is the cheapest way to avoid becoming one of them.
  6. Sell to the segment, not the function. The adoption gap is a firm-size gap[3][5][7][8]. Pricing, deployment (one cloud function, one database) and the first ten questions should be designed for a 50-person firm with no IT department.
