# Business case: what ThreadPilot is worth in hours and money

**Short answer (likely case, Singapore costs).** ThreadPilot takes over about **62 hours a month** of a planner's
preparation and call-back work (range 51 to 75), about a third of one person: **about S$18,400 a year**. Finding problems
through the morning briefing avoided about 30 working days of late delivery every two months in simulation; priced at half
of one retailer's real late penalty, that is **about S$13,100 a year** (S$7,700 to S$18,600). Running it costs **about
S$1,900 a year**. Net: **about S$29,600 a year**. A factory that instead hired a full-time planning assistant to give the
same on-call answers would pay about S$49,100 a year.

**What kind of numbers these are.** The time per task is an estimate built from the course brief (the planner's "half hour
every morning") and the tasks the brief lists, not a stopwatch study: no real planner has been timed. The lateness figure
comes from our simulator. The selling prices are assumptions, because the data has none. Every input sits in one place,
`INPUTS` in [`analysis/business_case.py`](../analysis/business_case.py), with its source, and the tables below are
generated from it. Change an input and re-run `python -m analysis.business_case`.

## 1. The planner's time

Today the general manager phones the planner, and the planner opens the spreadsheets: export the orders and the production
log, refresh the pivot tables, compare each stage with its usual level, look for orders that stopped moving, write it up,
and call back. With ThreadPilot the preparation is done by code before the manager asks; what is left for a person is
reading the briefing and acting on exceptions.

Each task has three guesses for its length (low, likely, high) and for how often it happens. The calculation draws 10,000
possible months from those ranges (a triangular distribution for each), so the result is a range, not one number.

## 2. What “about 30 working days of lateness avoided per two months” means

**In one sentence:** across all the orders a factory like ours handles in two months, finding problems through the morning
briefing instead of a weekly check would cut the total days those orders are late by about 30.

**How it was measured** ([business_value.md](business_value.md)):

1. Our simulator starts from the real data on 1 April and plays the factory forward, one working day at a time. In each
   two-month run it plants six disruptions at random: two customers whose orders all stop, two single orders that stop,
   and two production stages that slow down. The briefing code never sees the plan; it has to spot them in the data.
2. Each run is played three times with **exactly the same random events**, changing only when each problem is noticed:
   nobody acts; noticed **by hand** after 5 working days (a weekly review, or a customer calling); noticed **through the
   briefing** on the first morning it shows the problem. In both cases the problem is fixed 2 working days after it is
   noticed.
3. For every order in progress or arriving in those two months (about 88 orders a run), we add up how many working days
   it finished after its due date. One order 5 days late plus another 3 days late is 8 “days of lateness”.
4. We repeat this for 40 different random runs.

**The result:** total lateness was 544.5 working days a run when problems were noticed by hand, and 514.0 when they were
noticed through the briefing: **30.5 days less**, and **1.6 fewer late orders**, every two months.

**What “95%: 18 to 43” means:** the 40 runs did not all save the same amount; some had worse disruptions than others.
From their spread, we are 95% confident the true average saving lies between 18 and 43 working days. Even the low end is a
clear saving.

**Why it is not bigger:** about 30 days is roughly 5% of the total lateness. This simulated factory is overloaded, so some
lateness only moves from one order to another when a stuck order restarts. For the orders the problems actually hit, the
briefing helped in 37 of 40 runs.

**Why it depends on the routine it replaces:** the briefing's value is the time it saves in noticing. If people would
notice within 3 working days anyway, the saving is about 16 days; if they would take 10, about 37.

## 3. Pricing a day of lateness

Late delivery costs a garment factory real money: buyers deduct penalties (chargebacks), cancel orders, or the factory pays
for air freight to catch up. One public example: URBN (Urban Outfitters) charges suppliers **2% per day late (10% a week),
capped at 10% of the order's cost** ([URBN vendor chargebacks](https://vendor.urbn.com/us/chargebacks/chargeback-information)).
Industry write-ups put all compliance chargebacks at 2 to 5% of wholesale invoices
([Uphance](https://www.uphance.com/blog/retailer-chargebacks-deductions-apparel-wholesale-margin/)).

We use **half of URBN's rate, 1% of the order value per working day late**, and assumed selling prices (FOB, US$ per
piece): hoodie 9, cardigan 10, crewneck sweater 9, vest 7, polo 6, beanie 3, scarf 4. With the real order sizes in
`data/orders.csv` (779 pieces on average) the average order is worth about US$5,500, so one order one day late costs
about S$72. The data's own subcontract cost per piece (US$0.80 to 1.60 a piece at the workshops) is far below these
prices, as it should be: it covers one making step, not the garment.

The lateness saving scales with the order value: if the real prices are twice ours, so is that line.

## 4. Running ThreadPilot

- **OpenAI model** (GPT-4.1, US$2 per million input tokens and US$8 per million output tokens
  ([price listing](https://pricepertoken.com/pricing-page/model/openai-gpt-4.1))): two model calls per question, about
  4,000 tokens in and 600 out each, 25 questions a factory day.
- **Tencent CloudBase Standard plan**, ¥199 a month ([CloudBase pricing](https://cloudbase.cloud.tencent.com/en/pricing));
  the Personal plan is ¥19.9.
- **Upkeep**, 4 hours a month of someone's time to check the system and the data feed.
- **Not included:** building it (our project), or the GM's own time saved waiting for call-backs, which is real but harder
  to price.

## 5. People costs used

- Planner: S$48,171 a year average base salary for a production planner in Singapore
  ([Payscale](https://www.payscale.com/research/SG/Job=Production_Planner%2C_Manufacturing/Salary), updated Feb 2024), plus
  17% employer CPF: S$4,697 a month, S$24.6 an hour over a 44-hour week.
- Full-time planning assistant, for comparison: S$3,500 a month (assumption, the low end of the planner range) plus CPF:
  S$4,095 a month, about S$49,100 a year.

If the factory is in a lower-wage country, the labour line shrinks roughly in proportion; the lateness line does not,
because buyers' penalties are on the order's value.

## Limits

- **No real planner was timed and no real manager has used ThreadPilot.** The time table is our estimate. The next step is
  to time one planner for a week, with and without it.
- **The lateness saving is from our own simulator**, with problems of the kinds the briefing was built to find.
- **Selling prices and the penalty rate are assumptions.** We used half of a real retailer's rate to stay conservative.
- **The year figures multiply two simulated months by six**, assuming disruptions keep happening at the same rate.
