# What is the co-pilot worth? A paired simulation

**Short answer, in simulation only.** When six disruptions hit the factory over two months (two customers whose orders all stop,
two single orders that stop, two stages that slow down), finding them through the morning briefing instead of by the usual
routine avoided about **30 working days of lateness** per simulated future (95% interval 18 to 43), out of about 545: roughly
**3.5% less lateness and 1.6 fewer late orders**. For the customers and orders the disruptions actually hit, it helped in 37 of
40 futures. How much it is worth depends on how slow the usual routine is: from about 16 days if problems would be noticed within 3
working days anyway, to about 46 days if they would take 10.

**What this is not.** It is not "time saved", which the brief names as the business impact and which needs a real general manager
using the system; nobody has. It is the next best thing that can be measured without one: the same simulated factory, the same
disruptions and the same random draws, run with the problems found three ways.

## Why a paired simulation

Comparing two different simulated futures would mix the co-pilot's effect with luck (one future simply had worse disruptions).
Here every future is run three times with identical random draws, and only the moment each disruption is noticed changes:

* **Nobody acts**: every disruption runs its full length.
* **Found by hand**: each disruption is noticed a fixed number of working days after it starts (5 in the main result: a weekly review,
  or a customer calling to ask), then ends 2 working days later (the chase, the repair).
* **Found by the co-pilot**: each disruption is noticed on the first morning the briefing shows it in its top five findings (decided by
  code, below), then ends 2 working days later. A disruption the briefing never shows, or shows later than the routine would, is
  noticed by hand, so the co-pilot can only add information, never hide it.

The difference between the last two, in the same future, is the co-pilot's effect. A finding that was already on the page before the
disruption began does not count as finding it, so an order that was quiet for other reasons earns no credit.

## Method

* **Futures.** 40, each from its own random seed, rolled forward from 1 April by the simulator (`simulation/simulate.py`) for 260 days.
* **Disruptions,** planted at random times in the first 45 days: two customer holds (10 to 20 days), two order holds (10 to 20 days),
  two stage slowdowns (output cut to 45 to 75% for 4 to 10 days). The briefing code never reads them; it has to find them in the data.
* **Matching a finding to a disruption.** A customer hold: `customer_stalled:<customer>` or a quiet-order finding naming one of its
  orders. An order hold: a quiet-order finding naming it, or its customer stalling. A stage slowdown: `stage_below:<stage>`, raised by
  the 3-sigma + CUSUM rule chosen in [spc_detection.md](spc_detection.md).
* **Outcome.** Working days late, summed over every order in progress on 1 April or arriving in the first 60 days, each followed until
  it finished.

Reproduce: `python -m analysis.business_value` (about two minutes). A test (`tests/test_business_value.py`) re-renders the tables below
from the saved results and checks the paired design on one future.

## How to read it

* **Where the value comes from.** A customer whose orders all stop is shown within about 2 working days, a slow stage usually the
  next morning. A single order going quiet takes as long to show as the routine would (median 5 working days), because one idle order is
  only flagged when later-due orders visibly pass it. So the gain is mostly from customers and stages.
* **Why the co-pilot was sometimes worse.** This factory is overloaded and works earliest-due-first. Releasing a held order sooner
  puts it back in the queue ahead of others, so some lateness moves rather than disappears, and in 7 of 40 futures the total went up a
  little. For the orders the disruptions actually stopped, it was better in 37 of 40. Total lateness falls only when capacity comes
  back, which is why the stage slowdowns matter most.
* **Why "found by hand" is sometimes worse than "nobody acts"** (in 11 of 40 futures). The same effect: ending a hold early in a full
  factory can push other orders past their dates. It is a property of the queue, not of the method, and it is why the comparison is always paired.

## Limits

* **Every number depends on the assumed lags.** The table under "How the result moves" is the honest answer: the co-pilot's value is the
  time it saves in noticing, so it is worth more where problems are noticed late today.
* **Acting on a false alarm costs time, and that is not modelled.** The study in [spc_detection.md](spc_detection.md) puts the stage
  false alarms at about 2 a month.
* **The simulator is not a real factory**, and it has no partial progress, overtime or outside workshops.
* **Only six kinds of disruption are planted**, all of which the briefing was designed to find. Problems it was not designed for would
  show no gain.
